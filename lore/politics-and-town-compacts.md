# Who keeps the road open

Status: proposed political lore and future gameplay direction. The Endless World and player guild membership are established. Current guards enforce town property; councils, treaties, diplomatic choices, and wars described here are not implemented. Quoted passages are player-facing samples.

## Neighbors in an infinite world

The collapse brought together peoples who had never shared a border. A royal boundary stone might now stand beside a village whose ancestors never had a king. Both communities need the same bridge. Neither agrees that the other's old map settles who should repair it.

There is no government for the whole Endless World. Most people know the towns within a practical journey of home. Some settlements stand alone. Others belong to leagues, kingdoms, or agreements inherited from former worlds. Those agreements survive where people still recognize them.

Infinity does not put a second well beside every dry village. Water, fertile ground, shelter, and safe crossings matter where people can reach them. A road through dangerous country can be worth more than a distant stretch of empty land. These local dependencies give politics a reason beyond claiming ever larger borders.

## Who governs a town

Use a few government traditions, then give each actual settlement a particular history and disagreement. Its origin, visual style, and residents' ancestry do not determine whether it is fair or cruel. Residents of the same town can support different leaders.

| Proposed tradition | Who makes decisions | A local pressure worth writing about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common council | Households choose speakers for public work | A new neighborhood needs a voice before the older households will share the well |
| Trade charter | Trades and market households negotiate a charter | Merchants pay for a bridge and want the right to charge everyone using it |
| Stewardship | A steward holds inherited duties, with local customs limiting the office | An heir recognizes an old-world boundary that now cuts through a neighbor's farm |
| Keeper compact | Households entrust the fountain and refuge to a keeper and a public assembly | A keeper wants to shelter arrivals whom the assembly fears |
| Road league | Several towns share crossing, escort, and supply obligations | One member receives travelers while another pays most of the watch's wages |

Town archetypes can suggest likely work without fixing the government. A caravan town can have a household council. A woodland community can employ a steward. Write the disagreement and the people affected before selecting heraldry or titles.

### Customs for the proposed additional town identities

Townwright's additional settlement identities can carry these ordinary customs. These are writing prompts, not implemented government selection or type-specific dialogue. Preserve each actual town's identity and coherent visual family. A name such as Clockwork Ward does not establish working machines; a future magical or architectural form still needs its own support.

| Identity | Proposed custom | A possible local disagreement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grain Commons | Keep a cup ready for an arrival | Who contributes provisions when more travelers stay |
| Quarry Borough | Name stones that have been repaired and reused | Whether the original mason or the repairing household receives the credit |
| Lantern Court | Lend a lamp to a neighbor going out after supper | Who replaces a damaged borrowed lamp |
| Archive Steps | Copy disagreeing accounts onto facing pages | Which account deserves the scarce dry shelf |
| Rootbound Ward | Ask neighbors before pruning shared roots | Whose doorway a root blocks, and who benefits from its shade |
| Herbkeepers Common | Give a new household a cutting | Whether a valuable remedy should have a price |
| Glasswater | Shade a visitor's water jar before asking for news | How much water local trades may use when supplies are low |
| Clockwork Ward | Use a human caller when the clocks disagree | Who decides when the market closes |
| Pilgrim Court | Offer shelter to travelers of different faiths | Whether one congregation may claim the common meeting space |
| Orchard Commons | Leave part of the harvest for late arrivals | Who decides how much the households can spare |
| Aurora Watch | Wrap bells while travelers are overdue | How long to keep a watch on a costly winter route |
| Bellkeepers Hearth | Unwrap a bell when its traveler returns | Who should speak for a household still waiting |

These habits can survive from former worlds or develop after the collapse. Residents can explain which they believe happened without requiring a universal answer. Local religion remains open; Pilgrim Court does not adopt an external pantheon or prove a particular faith correct.

## Does every town have guards?

In the current game, each active town supplies two watch actors. They patrol local approaches and enforce that town's property claims. There is no implemented choice of government or defense arrangement, and these guards are not ordinary killable enemies.

For future lore, every sanctuary settlement should have a way to uphold hospitality and settle immediate trouble. That need does not require an identical paid garrison everywhere. A small common can share watch duty among neighbors; a busy market can pay a permanent watch; members of a road league can share patrols. Larger places can have several civic offices.

Guard appearance, names, and dialogue can express those differences before adding new behavior. A watch member might be a bridgewright on duty this week. Another may have served for twenty years and know every missing crate by its owner.

Proposed common-watch line:

> I mend the bridge on ordinary days. Today I keep the steps clear. Please give me an ordinary day.

Keep town defense distinct from private faction membership. Hearthkeepers tend hospitality; Milewardens travel and investigate. Neither group automatically commands the watch. Shared custom can make the fountain a place for negotiation while the town still enforces its property law. Guild membership gives the player no exemption from that law.

Current sanctuary safety concerns monsters. Refusing to return stolen town property can still provoke the watch. Future political writing must preserve that distinction. A watchless settlement needs an explicit law, safety, and restitution design before it can replace a guarded generated town.

## Are towns at war?

Some former worlds brought old rivalries with them. The wider setting can contain wars, ceasefires, disputed borders, and communities still rebuilding. Most ordinary journeys should also find trade partners, indifferent neighbors, and peaceful disagreements. A permanent war between every adjacent pair would leave little room for hospitality or exploration.

Current gameplay has no town-versus-town war simulation. Introduce relations first through accounts, shared projects, and local requests. Escalate only particular disputes with authored stakes and supported outcomes.

| Relationship | What a traveler might see | First story opportunity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shared compact | Matching repair marks on a crossing; households sharing supplies | Discover who was left out of the agreement |
| Commercial rivalry | Two markets claim the same road name; conflicting notices | Compare an old charter with the people doing the work now |
| Unsettled grievance | A returned relic has two claimants; a neighbor refuses a public invitation | Record both accounts and arrange a meeting once meeting outcomes exist |
| Embargo or broken pact | Delayed shipments and arguments at a supply yard | Find which obligation failed; restricted trade needs real transaction rules |
| Ceasefire | An old checkpoint hosts a meal for both sides | Recover a missing clause without assuming either witness tells the whole story |
| Local war | Damaged approaches, displaced households, and organized forces | Survey a safe refuge route after combat, refuge, and route-state handlers exist |

An old-world border does not settle present ownership. Give each conflict a concrete cause: a crossing, access to water, a damaged working, a vanished person, or a treaty interpreted differently after the collapse. Avoid assigning virtue by species or former world. An ambitious councilor can be wrong while their town's need is real.

## A Milewarden's political problem

Maps matter to people who move goods, find shelter, collect tolls, or march an army. A truthful account can help more than one of them. Guild chapters can disagree over what to publish and who should receive a copy.

The proposed guild charter asks members to distinguish observations from testimony, respect hosts, and record a safe return route. Chapters decide local disputes; there is no authority that instantly tells every distant member what the player did. The guild can offer mediation because it travels between communities, but mediation does not make it sovereign.

Proposed chapter conversation:

> The miller wants the bridge on every map. The keeper wants the refuge left off them. Both gave you supper. Tell me what you promised before we make another copy.

A meaningful political choice should name the affected people and the likely consequence. Start with how an account is presented and who receives it. Add material effects only when their handlers exist. Disagreement can leave a relationship strained without turning every guard into an enemy.

## First political story: both ends of the bridge

Two neighboring towns repair opposite ends of one crossing. One uses a toll charter copied from a former kingdom. The other remembers a compact promising passage in exchange for maintenance. A Milewarden is asked to compare the records.

The first version can use the existing evidence-comparison loop: learn the local account, reach a real archive, copy the other account, and report the disagreement. Both texts must contain enough information to support the finding. Existing reward and return behavior remain sufficient.

A later version can support a mediated repair agreement, a revised toll compact, or leaving the dispute unresolved. Each option needs saved local consequences, a clear explanation, and a usable travel route. A truthful report and the player's preferred settlement are separate decisions; do not require a false historical answer to support one side.

## Authoring and implementation boundaries

Start with one pair of real settlements and one shared dependency. Store their relationship once, so residents on both sides refer to the same dispute. Local allegations can differ; the treaty, physical crossing, and actual outcome need one consistent record. Avoid giving every region a mandatory war or enumerating political relations across the infinite world.

Future government and watch profiles can be additive settlement metadata if they do not change existing law behavior. Existing debts, stolen-object provenance, accepted quests, collected passages, and route targets must retain their identities. Do not convert a town to a new political state merely because it reloaded.

Gate embargos, checkpoints, patrol hostility, destruction, refugees, and war progression behind actual behaviors with saved state and visible warnings. Sanctuary towns and the player's home remain reliable recovery places. No offscreen political event should destroy the player's base, close their only return route, or consume a reward while they are away.

The [roadmap](../docs/ENDLESS_WORLD_ROADMAP.md) begins with local political voice and a comparison story. Dynamic conflict and war are later evaluation work, after relationship and route consequences are usable and tested.
