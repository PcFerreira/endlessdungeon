# The unfinished maps

The Endless World and the player's Milewarden membership are adopted lore. The guild's detailed history, chapter houses, faction conflicts, and expedition stories on this page are proposals. Quoted passages are player-facing writing samples; the design sections are for authors and developers.

## A guild for a world nobody can finish

The first Milewardens carried maps that ended at the borders of vanished worlds. They marked where the roads now met, who offered shelter, and which old names still meant something to the people using them. Others copied their work and carried it farther.

Their guild grew along those roads. One chapter keeps its maps in a tower; another borrows the back room of an inn. A member can arrive with a wet journal and find someone willing to dry its pages before asking what they say.

The guild has no final map. Its members catalogue strange creatures, buildings, customs, and workings as well as roads. They often disagree. A good account includes the person who told it, the place where it was found, and what the writer still wants to know.

Proposed chapter-house inscription:

> We keep a place for the traveler and a shelf for the account.
>
> Write what you saw. Name who told you the rest.
>
> Leave room for the next hand.

Keep the guild human in scale. An infinite world has chapters that have never heard of one another, old journals awaiting a reader, and routes that need another walk. Membership gives the player companions and a purpose. The player's ancestry, class, chosen name, and private reasons for traveling remain theirs.

## More factions, or more names?

Develop the four existing organizations before adding more guilds. Give them distinct responsibilities and disagreements that affect a particular place. Initially the player remains a Milewarden; meeting another faction does not require joining a second progression system.

| Group | What matters to them | What they ask of a Milewarden | Where they disagree |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Milewardens | Useful routes and accounts others can examine | Investigate, compare, map, and bring news home | A widely shared discovery can endanger a place that welcomed the surveyor |
| Hearthkeepers | Hospitality, safe towns, and the people using them | Find a missing source of water; learn what threatens a refuge | A route to a sheltered community may belong in a keeper's private book |
| The Small Repairs | Workmanship, repair, and the wishes of living objects | Learn what a relic does before taking it apart | Salvage can destroy a useful working or take a person from their home |
| The Unclosed Office | The old atlas and the places it promised to preserve | Recover a page; determine what an old boundary meant | Some insist a surviving map outranks the neighbors who now live there |

Each group needs people who disagree with their own colleagues. A Hearthkeeper might welcome a published road that another fears. A mender can need the money from salvage and still object to a careless buyer. The Office can include descendants trying to understand an inherited duty alongside hostile remnants still enforcing it.

These organizations cross political borders; they do not replace town governments. [Politics and town compacts](politics-and-town-compacts.md) develops the councils, watches, local agreements, and disputes that guild members encounter.

Avoid a single good-versus-evil faction meter. Proposed consequences belong to the relevant chapter, household, working, or route. The player should know who benefits, what could be lost, and whether a decision can be reconsidered. New factions earn a place when they offer a new kind of relationship or decision that these groups cannot carry.

## Places to return to

Guild bases should be public chapter houses attached to real settlements. They can share an existing inn, archive, or workshop before needing a dedicated building. Keep the Sanctum as the player's private home; a chapter house is where other travelers keep their maps and wait for news.

A first chapter needs a recognizable custodian, a shared map table, an expedition shelf, and a place for the player's returned account. These can begin as one ordinary room. Later chapters should differ in people and work, not only wall materials. A winter chapter worries about shelter routes; a market chapter argues over which discovery its traders will turn into merchandise.

Proposed welcome:

> Put the journal here, away from the kettle. We lost a coastline that way last winter.
>
> There's a place at supper. Tell us what you found when you've eaten.

Begin with a charter to read and a resident who recognizes a guildmate. Then add supported report exchanges and a visible record of one completed expedition. Storage, rest, respawn, fast travel, training, and paid services need separate behavior; the chapter's appearance alone promises none of them.

The player's best souvenirs can be copies, sketches, and a local thank-you. Let some appear on a chapter shelf or at home after a real outcome. A guild does not need to demand a sack of twenty identical objects to notice useful work.

## Mysteries left by the collapse

The collapse is a known part of the world. Questions concern particular lives and places: what survived, what joined what, and who has lived with the result. Avoid postponing every answer behind a universal secret about the Dawn.

The examples below require authored sites and supported objectives before becoming quests. They should use the existing local-comparison behavior for a first slice where possible. A later slice can add another handler once it has a clear outcome and a return path.

| Proposed mystery | What the player notices | Investigation and human stake | Resolution to design |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The bridge with two beginnings | Opposite ends carry different builders' names and incompatible dates | Copy both inscriptions and hear from bridgewright households whose former worlds used different calendars | Both accounts belong on the map; neither family needs to surrender its name. Begin with a comparison, without inventing calendar-changing magic |
| The mill beneath a foreign sky | An old tower's ceiling depicts two moons; a repaired wheel serves the present town | Discover how a working from one former world became useful to its new neighbors | Publish its history and record who maintains it. Physical repair and interrupted supply are later objectives |
| The road nobody advertises | A keeper has removed a refuge's name from a public copy | Ask the residents why they fear visitors, and examine the real approach | Offer a public name, a restricted account, or a safety warning once map disclosure has a working handler; a hidden destination must still be physically discoverable |
| The kettle that signed the inventory | A repair ledger includes an ordinary-looking vessel's chosen name | Compare the entry with its household's account; find out whether someone treated a neighbor as a possession | First record the disagreement. A speaking Potfolk encounter and any relocation require explicit character support and consent |

Start with one mystery players can resolve and remember. Give it an ordinary opening, one surprising observation, a question worth following, and an answer that changes their understanding of the place. Add a harder expedition after that loop works. A funny note can coexist with danger and loss; no discovery needs to end with a joke.

## Expeditions and homecomings

The repeatable promise is a place to investigate and someone interested in the result. An expedition begins with a question tied to a real destination. The approach offers a visible cue or a local account. Inside, the player finds evidence while navigating danger. Returning lets someone respond to what was learned, and the journal keeps enough detail to recognize the place on a later journey.

Use existing books, conversations, roadside notes, journal records, and comparison requests for the first loop. Later, add a route survey or a limited relic investigation as distinct objective types. Combat remains a possible cost of reaching evidence; it need not be the purpose of every guild request.

Reward useful knowledge with a response, a fuller map, a display copy, or a supported local opportunity. Equipment and currency remain available where appropriate. A report should identify a place or finding, so repeated copies cannot earn unlimited rewards. Let abandoned requests wait without an expiring real-world deadline.

## Authoring boundaries

Keep the same underlying place in the physical world, its map, its clue, and its residents' accounts. An unverified rumor can be wrong about history while still naming a real destination. Do not claim the player's journal contains a discovery they have yet to make.

Former worlds explain different local visual traditions. Give each settlement and biome a coherent family of materials and construction. Show the meeting of traditions at a deliberate boundary, repair, or shared building rather than random prop mixtures. Neither a change of style nor an old-world memory automatically changes traversal or combat rules.

Implement chapter and expedition identities, report outcomes, map disclosure, and saved local relationships before authoring passages that depend on those states. Preserve already collected editions and accepted requests. The [roadmap](../docs/ENDLESS_WORLD_ROADMAP.md) orders these proposals after the current geography and discovery work.
